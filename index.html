Why the dollar alone tells you nothing

$8,000 rent, $100,000 month
8,000 ÷ 100,000 = 8%. Comfortable. Room to pay everyone and still keep something.
$8,000 rent, $40,000 month
8,000 ÷ 40,000 = 20%. Same lease, now eating you alive.

The rent never changed. The month got smaller. That is why every target on the next tab is a percentage and not a dollar.

Every number, worked out on one month

A $100,000 month · $85,000 service · $15,000 retail

Here is each target with the actual division. Cover the answer and try one yourself.

Payroll
$45,000 paid to people ÷ $100,000 month = 45%
Everyone on the payroll report, you included. Ceiling is 45%. Employer taxes are not in this number, they get their own line.
Rent
$8,000 rent ÷ $100,000 month = 8%
Ceiling is 10%. Rent is fixed, so the only way this number falls is if the month gets bigger.
Your own pay
$10,000 to you ÷ $100,000 month = 10%
Floor is 10%. This is ten of the 45 above, not an extra ten on top. You are on the payroll.
Profit
$20,000 left ÷ $100,000 month = 20%
Floor is 20%. This is what survives after every other line is paid.
Everything else
$25,000 of bills ÷ $100,000 month = 25%
Product, card fees, insurance, utilities, software, marketing, payroll taxes, repairs. No target of its own. It is whatever is left once the other three are right.

45 + 10 + 25 + 20 = 100. That is the whole month. There is never more than 100% to hand out, so every dollar you add to one line comes off another.

Retail is the one that trips everybody, including me

There are two retail percentages. They are both correct. They answer different questions, and mixing them up is why your number never matches anybody else's.

Say you did $80,000 service and $20,000 retail

How much of my business is retail?
$20,000 ÷ $100,000 total
20% of everything you sold was product.
How well is my team selling retail?
$20,000 ÷ $80,000 service
25% retail for every dollar of service.

The second one is the benchmark. Target is 15% or better, so 25% is strong. It is measured against service because retail rides along with service visits. You are asking: for every $100 of service we did, how much product went out the door with it?

Put retail on the bottom too and you are partly measuring retail against itself, which mutes the answer. On these numbers it drops from 25% to 20% and a salon that is doing well starts to look average.

Backbar works the same way. Divide it by service, not by the whole month. $4,250 of colour ÷ $85,000 of service = 5%, right on target. Divide by the whole $100,000 instead and you get 4.2%, which looks better than it is and hides a leak.

Retail-to-service · backbar

Two more have their own bottom number entirely. Payroll tax divides by payroll, not revenue. Retail replenishment divides by retail sales. When a number looks wrong, check the bottom of the fraction first.

Pull your own four numbers

Five minutes, once a month, all of it in your booking software and your bank.

  1. Total revenue. Everything that came in. Service plus retail.
  2. Total payroll. Commissions, hourly, salary, front desk, bonuses, your own pay. One number.
  3. Your fixed bills. Rent, insurance, utilities, software, marketing.
  4. Product. What backbar cost you, and what the retail you sold cost you.

Use last month, not a good month. The point is to see what is actually happening.

A floor, not a goal. Below 10% is survival mode.
Two limits that live INSIDE the 45%
  • Your pay: at least 10% of income. If this is zero, the salon is a hobby.
  • Manager and admin: 10% or less of income. Salaried, not behind a chair.

Read that as 10 of the 45, not 45 and then another 10. Both come out of the same block. Add them on top and you have quietly budgeted 55% of every dollar to people, which is where the profit went.

The grey slice is not a target. It is simply whatever is left once the other three are right, and it shrinks every time you beat 20% profit.
